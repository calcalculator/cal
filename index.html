The bible — Russo's rules

Distilled from the six-part memoir and his comment replies (simon-russo.com). Paraphrased — read the originals; the stories carry what the rules can't.

I. R — the center of the universe
  1. R is a fixed percentage of the portfolio and scales with AUM. Once set, positions are denominated in method units, never dollars. The dollar figure is banned from the decision room.
  2. R expresses exactly one thing: how much edge you believe is in front of you (the tier). The moment R becomes a lever to manufacture the size you want, the outcome is driving the risk input — backwards.
  3. Geometric drawdown risk scales with risk as a % of AUM, not the dollar amount. 1% at a small account and 1% at a huge one are mathematically identical — the number only looks scarier.
  4. Survival levers live outside per-trade R, on purpose: how much heat you'll carry at once (heat cap), and when you stop trading in a bad stretch (cold-streak brake). Never blend them into sizing.
  5. What matters in the end: EV positive, how positive, whether you behave, and whether sizing keeps you in the game long enough for the math to work.
II. Stops
  1. The stop lives at invalidation — the price where the idea is plainly wrong. On volatile names that's often far wider than a "tight" stop, and the win rate holds because of the width.
  2. Stops are never placed once, and move both ways: enter deliberately loose, ratchet in as price action confirms; if the name falls back into noise, widening back to honest invalidation is correct.
  3. Once the stop is where it belongs, share count flexes to hold dollar risk at R — so "undersized" cannot happen. Feeling undersized at a logical stop is the mirror, not a sizing problem.
  4. Never place a stop to defend a win rate. Win rate is an output — move it through the inputs (selection, stop logic, execution), never by protecting the number directly.
  5. A canonized stop should never appear in your rearview mirror. If the setup called for a stop by now, it isn't the setup anymore.
  6. Losses that "ran a little" — cut at 1.8R or 2.5R instead of the planned 1R — are quietly lethal to EV. The danger is rarely the catastrophe; it's the small trespass repeated.
  7. Move a stop to breakeven only because something contextually changed in the market that makes the risk no longer worth carrying — never to feel safe or to avoid losing dollars. The goal once a trade works isn't to cover risk; it's to compound and press the winner as far as the market allows. Trade the chart, not your P/L.
  8. Why width IS the win rate (interpretation): your realized win rate is not your thesis win rate — it's the probability the setup resolves before the stop gets touched. A stop inside ordinary noise converts correct theses into losses; those losses carry zero information about your edge — they're manufactured by placement. Width at honest invalidation closes that gap, and R-sizing makes it free in dollars (wider stop → fewer shares → same 1R). What width costs in R-multiple, the ratchet restores: wide early when you need immunity, tight late when you need asymmetry — width and tightness are a schedule, not a choice.
  9. The noise-stopout audit (interpretation): tag every loss as invalidation (the thesis actually broke) or noise stopout (stopped, then the setup resolved without you). The system is engineered so the second category approaches zero. If your journal fills with noise stopouts, your stops are inside the noise and you are paying for tightness with your win rate. The ADR check (1.5+ daily ranges to the stop) is the pre-trade version of this audit.
  10. The thesis dictates the width (interpretation): stop distance is the thesis's own claim about path and speed, priced. A violent-move-now thesis carries its invalidation close — in price and in time — because if the move doesn't come fast, the idea is already wrong; honest tightness, and R-sizing hands you the larger size that goes with it (his defect-moment entries worked this way: the unhalt either repriced immediately or the read was dead). A destination-over-time thesis carries invalidation at distant structure — enter loose, take the smaller size, and let the ratchet and pyramid build the position as the path confirms. Width is never aggression or timidity. The test on every tight stop remains the honest question: is this where the idea is wrong — or where my desired size wants it to be?
III. Adds & pyramiding
  1. Constant-risk pyramiding: at every add, compute total target shares from the risk budget and the distance to the new stop; the add is target minus current. The focus is never increasing risk — it's keeping it the same while size grows.
  2. Booked profit expands the budget: if 1R is $100 and you've banked $50 on the sequence, $150 now finances the next decision ("trading around a core" / recycling).
  3. The add trigger is a price-based or time-based signal read from structure — never an impulse. Position placed, then: watch. Time brings information.
  4. The standing heuristic mid-trade: what would I do if I had no position?
  5. His confirmation of the full chain: no signal → no stop movement → no adds. Wait for the market's go-ahead; otherwise, wait. (And opening with smaller risk to test the waters is a legitimate stylistic choice — up to you.)
IV. Exits & trims
  1. Exit when the trade has actually worked — never because the P/L number got big enough to feel like a house. Dollar-discomfort is not a signal.
  2. The wiggle rule for trims: ask, will this be at this price or better later? If you're confident the answer is yes, take a good amount off.
  3. Exits are decided at entry and pre-placed. The good deaths are when price walks to your waiting exits; the disasters all start with holding an hour past what the plan said.
V. Selection & win rate
  1. Win rate is engineered through selectivity. Trading everything that looks vaguely right mechanically lowers it; genuinely waiting for the setup that meets a high bar raises it. Patience is a win-rate lever.
  2. Refusing wrong trades and giving right ones room are the same discipline seen from two ends: only take setups whose logical stop you can respect, then respect it wherever it sits.
  3. Interrogate your filters — the best setup in the arsenal may be labeled "avoid." Binary categories (long/short, threat/safe) are inherited, not real.
  4. Avoid Day One on parabolic movers. The rule that's easy to know and hard to obey.
  5. Most setups are replicas with bloodlines. Build the database; know today's setup by its resemblance to the family album.
  6. Labels and tags (from scanners, databases, conviction layers) are starting points, not verdicts — assigned by past-you or by software from a compressed rule. The chart in front of you is its own thing. Tools that label less are better than tools that label more.
  7. The fix is a gradient, not abolition: categories stay useful — what has to go is their monopoly on the answer. In real time, replace the name with a question: what's actually here? (Volume, prior range, relative move, spread, time of day.)
  8. His analysis stack, in his words: nothing fancy — price, volume, and pattern recognition. The edge is upstream of tooling.
VI. Rides, discipline & penalties
  1. The heroic "insane ride" is never required. Every big drawdown-ride either ended worse than the process path, or produced a win that process would have produced anyway without the -20R detour. The ride is a tax paid for refusing to admit the entry was wrong — and the graveyard of traders who rode the same way is silent.
  2. A trade is a commitment, not a prediction. Predictions adjusted along the way are unfalsifiable; commitments have hinges built in before entry.
  3. Build the penalty into the process before the market charges you a worse one. Deadlines without teeth are suggestions. Hard now, or harder later.
  4. Discipline's end state is the "state of grace": enough reps with enough consequences that the right thing stops being the hard thing. Both error and mastery are thoughtless technique — the difference is which master they serve.
  5. Holding through the second half of a trade because you held through the first half is not a thesis.
VII. The mirror & the feedback loop
  1. Re-trade reviews: run finished trades back through Cal and compare the optimal decision path to what you actually did, at every decision point. The gaps are the curriculum.
  2. An unjournaled decision is a conversation you're avoiding with yourself — and journal entries that measure nothing about your intention to change teach nothing. Dodo-bird journaling ("good day today!") is blindness with a diary.
  3. Feedback loops must exist before the regime change: logged trades, tracked metrics, regular review. The mind protects its models; the architecture has to out-vote it.
  4. Measure the unrealized parameters of your system, not just realized results. A high win rate can be ruin sleeping in the next room.
  5. Crowds infect, they don't instruct. Build in silence; the edge lives in stores of work — the databases, journals, and tagged screenshots no one will ever see.
  6. Watch what happens in you when the process feels uncomfortable. That discomfort is information about you, not about the trade. The market hands you the size you deserve.
  7. Audit your labels in review: sit with a traded chart and ask where a name foreclosed a possibility — where "breakout" stopped you from seeing, where "avoid" made you miss the long. The backward work bleeds forward; you can't refuse to name a chart in real time, but you can audit the naming afterward. You never reach unmediated seeing — you build a working relationship with a filter you can't turn off.
  8. Stay skeptical of your own edge — he never fully believed in his. The variables (timing, neurology, regime) are too entangled to separate, and at some point the skepticism stops being a problem and becomes the most honest position available. There was also never a moment the searching "ended": the signal just got too loud to rationalize away.

Sources: the memoir parts 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and his replies in the comment sections of Parts 1, 2, and 3 (Parts 4–6 have no author replies as of Aug 2026).
